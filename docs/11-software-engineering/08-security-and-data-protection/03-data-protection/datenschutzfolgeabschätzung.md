# DATA PROTECTION IMPACT ASSESSMENT (DPIA)

## Introduction

A Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) is a systematic process used to identify, assess and mitigate risks associated with the processing of personal data.

It is an important instrument of data protection and privacy management under the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). Its primary objective is to identify potential risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons before processing activities are carried out.

A DPIA is particularly relevant when organizations introduce new technologies, process large amounts of personal data or perform activities that may significantly affect individuals.

Examples include:

- Processing sensitive personal data.
- Systematic monitoring of individuals.
- Large-scale processing of personal data.
- Automated decision-making and profiling.
- Biometric identification systems.
- Processing personal data using new or emerging technologies.

The DPIA follows a risk-based approach. Rather than simply documenting which personal data is collected, it evaluates how processing activities may affect individuals and determines appropriate measures to reduce identified risks.

## Legal Foundations

### General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)

The legal foundation for Data Protection Impact Assessments is Article 35 of the GDPR.

According to Article 35(1), a DPIA is required when a type of processing, particularly when using new technologies, is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.

The assessment must be carried out before the processing begins.

The GDPR establishes several important requirements:

- The assessment must describe the envisaged processing operations and their purposes.
- The necessity and proportionality of the processing must be evaluated.
- Risks to the rights and freedoms of data subjects must be assessed.
- Measures to address identified risks must be defined.

Article 35(7) specifies the minimum content of a DPIA.

### Article 35 GDPR

Article 35(7) defines four essential components:

**Description of Processing Operations**

A systematic description of the envisaged processing operations and their purposes must be provided.

**Assessment of Necessity and Proportionality**

The processing activities must be evaluated in terms of whether they are necessary and proportionate to their intended purposes.

**Risk Assessment**

The potential risks to the rights and freedoms of data subjects must be identified and evaluated.

**Risk Mitigation Measures**

Appropriate technical and organizational measures must be identified to address the risks and demonstrate compliance with the GDPR.

### Article 36 GDPR: Prior Consultation

If a DPIA indicates that processing would result in a high risk in the absence of measures taken by the controller to mitigate the risk, and the controller cannot sufficiently reduce that risk, Article 36 GDPR requires prior consultation with the competent supervisory authority before processing begins.

The supervisory authority may provide advice and recommendations or exercise its corrective powers.

## When Is a DPIA Required?

Not every processing activity requires a DPIA.

The decisive factor is whether the processing is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.

Article 35(3) GDPR identifies three situations in which a DPIA is particularly required.

### Systematic and Extensive Evaluation

A DPIA is required when processing involves systematic and extensive evaluation of personal aspects relating to natural persons, based on automated processing, including profiling, and decisions that produce legal effects or similarly significantly affect individuals.

Examples include:

- Automated credit scoring.
- Automated recruitment systems.
- Behavioral profiling.
- Automated eligibility assessments.

### Large-Scale Processing of Special Categories of Data

A DPIA is required when processing special categories of personal data on a large scale, as defined in Article 9 GDPR.

These categories include:

- Racial or ethnic origin.
- Political opinions.
- Religious or philosophical beliefs.
- Trade union membership.
- Genetic data.
- Biometric data used for uniquely identifying a natural person.
- Health data.
- Data concerning a person's sex life or sexual orientation.

Personal data relating to criminal convictions and offences is separately regulated under Article 10 GDPR.

### Systematic Monitoring of Publicly Accessible Areas

A DPIA is required when processing involves systematic monitoring of publicly accessible areas on a large scale.

Examples include extensive video surveillance systems in public spaces.

### Additional Criteria

The European Data Protection Board (EDPB) and national supervisory authorities provide further criteria for identifying processing activities that may require a DPIA.

These include:

- Evaluation or scoring of individuals.
- Automated decision-making with significant effects.
- Systematic monitoring.
- Sensitive or highly personal data.
- Processing on a large scale.
- Matching or combining datasets.
- Processing involving vulnerable data subjects.
- Innovative use or application of new technological or organizational solutions.
- Processing that prevents individuals from exercising a right or using a service.

As a practical rule, processing activities that meet two or more of these criteria are generally more likely to require a DPIA. However, this is an indication rather than an absolute legal threshold.

## The DPIA Process

A DPIA is not simply a document that is completed once. It is a structured process that should be integrated into the development and implementation of data processing activities.

The process can be divided into several stages.

### Identification and Screening

The first step is to determine whether a DPIA is necessary.

Organizations should examine the intended processing activities and identify potential risks.

Important questions include:

- What personal data is being processed?
- Who are the affected individuals?
- What is the purpose of the processing?
- Which technologies are being used?
- Does the processing involve sensitive personal data?
- Is the processing likely to result in high risks?

If a DPIA is not required, the organization should document the reasons for this decision.

### Description of Processing Activities

The next step is to document the processing operations in detail.

This includes identifying:

- The categories of personal data.
- The categories of data subjects.
- The purposes of processing.
- The legal basis for processing.
- Data recipients and third parties.
- Data storage locations.
- Data retention periods.
- Data transfers to third countries.
- Technical systems and infrastructure.

A data flow diagram can be useful to illustrate how personal data is collected, transmitted, stored, accessed and deleted.

The description should provide a clear understanding of the entire data processing lifecycle.

### Assessment of Necessity and Proportionality

The organization must evaluate whether the processing activities are necessary and proportionate in relation to their intended purposes.

This involves assessing whether:

- The processing has a clearly defined and legitimate purpose.
- The selected data is adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary.
- Less intrusive alternatives are available.
- Data retention periods are justified.
- Data subjects are sufficiently informed.
- Appropriate safeguards are implemented.

The principle of data minimization, defined in Article 5(1)(c) GDPR, is particularly important.

It requires organizations to limit the processing of personal data to what is necessary for the intended purpose.

### Risk Identification

The next stage is to identify potential risks to the rights and freedoms of data subjects.

Unlike a general IT security risk assessment, a DPIA focuses on risks to individuals rather than exclusively on risks to organizational assets.

Potential risks include:

**Confidentiality Risks**

Unauthorized individuals may gain access to personal data.

Examples:

- Data breaches.
- Unauthorized disclosure.
- Inadequate access controls.
- Accidental publication of personal information.

**Integrity Risks**

Personal data may be modified, manipulated or corrupted.

Examples:

- Incorrect personal records.
- Unauthorized modification of data.
- Manipulation of automated decision-making inputs.

**Availability Risks**

Individuals may lose access to their personal data or services that depend on it.

Examples:

- Ransomware attacks.
- System failures.
- Loss of access to essential personal records.

**Risks to Individual Rights and Freedoms**

Processing activities may also directly affect individuals in ways that go beyond traditional information security risks.

Examples include:

- Discrimination caused by automated decision-making.
- Identity theft.
- Financial losses.
- Reputational damage.
- Loss of confidentiality.
- Unlawful surveillance.
- Loss of control over personal information.
- Restriction of fundamental rights.

### Risk Assessment

After identifying potential risks, their likelihood and severity must be evaluated.

A common approach is to use a risk matrix.

Risk can be assessed using the following simplified formula:

$$
R=L\times I
$$

where:

- $R$ denotes the risk score.
- $L$ denotes the likelihood of occurrence.
- $I$ denotes the impact or severity.

For example, both likelihood and impact can be evaluated on a scale from 1 to 5.

$$
L,I\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}
$$

The resulting risk score ranges from:

$$
R\in[1,25]
$$

An illustrative classification could be:

| Risk Score | Risk Level |
|---|---|
| 1–4 | Low |
| 5–9 | Medium |
| 10–16 | High |
| 17–25 | Very High |

This numerical model is a practical assessment tool, not a classification prescribed by the GDPR.

The assessment should consider both the likelihood of a harmful event and the severity of its potential consequences for affected individuals.

Importantly, a low likelihood does not necessarily imply a low risk. An event with a very severe impact may still require significant attention.

### Risk Mitigation

Once risks have been identified and assessed, appropriate measures must be defined to reduce them.

These measures can be technical, organizational or procedural.

**Technical Measures**

- Encryption of personal data.
- Pseudonymization.
- Access control mechanisms.
- Multi-factor authentication.
- Secure data transmission.
- Regular security updates.
- Backup and recovery systems.
- Data anonymization where appropriate.

**Organizational Measures**

- Employee training.
- Internal data protection policies.
- Defined access responsibilities.
- Incident response procedures.
- Regular audits.
- Data retention and deletion policies.
- Vendor and processor management.

**Privacy by Design and Default**

Article 25 GDPR requires organizations to implement data protection by design and by default.

This means that privacy considerations should be integrated into the development and operation of systems from the beginning.

Examples include:

- Collecting only necessary personal data.
- Restricting access by default.
- Using privacy-preserving technologies.
- Limiting data retention.
- Implementing appropriate default privacy settings.

### Documentation and Review

The final step is to document the DPIA and ensure that the identified measures are implemented.

A DPIA should include:

- A description of processing operations.
- The purposes of processing.
- An assessment of necessity and proportionality.
- Identified risks and their evaluation.
- Planned mitigation measures.
- Residual risks.
- Decisions and responsibilities.
- Consultation with relevant stakeholders.

The DPIA should also be reviewed whenever there is a change in the nature, scope, context or purposes of the processing.

It is therefore an ongoing process rather than a one-time compliance exercise.

## Risk Treatment and Residual Risk

An important distinction in risk management is the difference between inherent risk and residual risk.

**Inherent Risk**

Inherent risk describes the level of risk before mitigation measures are implemented.

**Residual Risk**

Residual risk describes the remaining risk after the planned mitigation measures have been applied.

This distinction is important because security and privacy measures rarely eliminate every possible risk.

The relationship can be illustrated as:

$$
\text{Inherent Risk}
\rightarrow
\text{Mitigation Measures}
\rightarrow
\text{Residual Risk}
$$

For example, consider a system that stores sensitive health information.

Before implementing encryption and access controls, unauthorized access may represent a significant risk.

After implementing appropriate safeguards, the likelihood of unauthorized access may decrease.

However, the possibility of security incidents cannot be completely eliminated.

The remaining risk must therefore be evaluated and documented.

If the DPIA indicates that the processing would still result in a high risk in the absence of sufficient mitigating measures, prior consultation with the supervisory authority under Article 36 GDPR is required.

## Roles and Responsibilities

Several stakeholders may be involved in conducting a DPIA.

### Data Controller

The data controller is responsible for ensuring that a DPIA is carried out when required.

The controller determines the purposes and means of processing personal data.

### Data Protection Officer

Where an organization has appointed a Data Protection Officer (DPO), the DPO must be consulted when carrying out a DPIA.

The DPO provides advice and monitors compliance with data protection requirements.

### Data Processor

A data processor processes personal data on behalf of the controller.

Processors should assist controllers in fulfilling their obligations under the GDPR, including providing information and support necessary for the DPIA.

### Data Subjects

Where appropriate, the controller should seek the views of the affected data subjects or their representatives.

This helps identify potential consequences that may not be apparent from a purely technical or organizational perspective.

## DPIA vs. General Risk Assessment

A DPIA is related to general risk management but has a distinct legal and practical purpose.

| Aspect | DPIA | General Risk Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Primary focus | Rights and freedoms of individuals | Organizational risks and assets |
| Legal foundation | Article 35 GDPR | Depends on the applicable framework |
| Scope | Personal data processing | Potentially all organizational activities |
| Main objective | Identify and mitigate privacy risks | Identify and manage risks |
| Timing | Before high-risk processing begins | Depends on the risk management process |
| Main perspective | Data subjects | Organization and stakeholders |

A DPIA may incorporate elements of an information security risk assessment, but the two processes should not be treated as interchangeable.

For example, a system may be technically secure while still creating significant privacy risks through excessive data collection or discriminatory profiling.

## Practical Example: Automated Recruitment System

Consider an organization introducing an automated recruitment system that evaluates job applicants based on their CVs, professional experience and other personal information.

The system uses machine learning to calculate suitability scores and rank applicants.

Such processing may involve systematic and extensive evaluation of personal aspects and automated decision-making.

Depending on the circumstances, it may therefore trigger the requirement for a DPIA under Article 35 GDPR.

### Processing Description

The system collects and processes:

- Names and contact information.
- Employment history.
- Educational qualifications.
- Professional skills.
- Application documents.
- Automated suitability scores.

The purpose is to support recruitment decisions by identifying potentially suitable candidates.

### Risk Identification

Potential risks include:

- Discrimination against certain groups of applicants.
- Incorrect evaluation of qualifications.
- Lack of transparency regarding automated assessments.
- Unauthorized access to application documents.
- Excessive retention of applicant information.

### Risk Assessment

An illustrative assessment might look as follows:

| Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Risk Score |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Unauthorized access | 3 | 4 | 12 |
| Algorithmic discrimination | 4 | 5 | 20 |
| Incorrect candidate ranking | 3 | 4 | 12 |
| Excessive data retention | 3 | 3 | 9 |

The values are hypothetical and demonstrate the application of a simple risk scoring methodology.

### Risk Mitigation

Possible measures include:

- Encryption of applicant information.
- Strict role-based access controls.
- Regular testing for algorithmic bias.
- Human review of significant recruitment decisions.
- Clear information about the processing activities.
- Defined data retention and deletion periods.

### Residual Risk

After implementing these measures, the remaining risks must be reassessed.

For example, regular bias testing may reduce the likelihood of discriminatory outcomes, but it cannot guarantee that the system will always produce fair results.

The organization must therefore continuously monitor the system and review the effectiveness of its safeguards.

## Relationship to Other GDPR Principles

A DPIA is closely connected to several fundamental GDPR principles.

**Lawfulness, Fairness and Transparency (Article 5(1)(a))**

Processing must have a valid legal basis and must be fair and transparent to data subjects.

**Purpose Limitation (Article 5(1)(b))**

Personal data must be collected for specified, explicit and legitimate purposes.

**Data Minimization (Article 5(1)(c))**

Only personal data that is necessary for the intended purposes should be processed.

**Accuracy (Article 5(1)(d))**

Personal data must be accurate and kept up to date where necessary.

**Storage Limitation (Article 5(1)(e))**

Personal data should not be retained longer than necessary.

**Integrity and Confidentiality (Article 5(1)(f))**

Personal data must be processed using appropriate security measures.

**Accountability (Article 5(2))**

The controller is responsible for compliance with the GDPR and must be able to demonstrate it.

A DPIA helps organizations demonstrate accountability by documenting how privacy risks are identified, evaluated and addressed.

## Summary

A Data Protection Impact Assessment is a systematic process for identifying and mitigating risks associated with personal data processing.

Under Article 35 GDPR, a DPIA is required when processing is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.

The assessment includes a description of processing activities, an evaluation of necessity and proportionality, an assessment of potential risks and the definition of appropriate mitigation measures.

A central component is the evaluation of both inherent and residual risks.

A DPIA is not limited to technical security considerations. It must also address broader consequences for individuals, including discrimination, loss of privacy, financial damage and restrictions of fundamental rights.

Ultimately, a DPIA supports the principle of accountability and helps organizations integrate privacy considerations into their processes, systems and technologies from the beginning.

It is therefore an essential instrument for implementing data protection by design and ensuring responsible processing of personal data.
